# NOAA/NWS Raindrop Shape

Checked: 2026-09-26
Source: NOAA National Weather Service
URL: https://www.weather.gov/media/oun/newsletter/winter2007.pdf

## Relevant facts captured

- The familiar pointed teardrop icon is physically wrong for a falling raindrop.
- Small raindrops around 1 mm or less are close to spherical because surface tension dominates.
- Larger drops become flattened on the bottom as aerodynamic pressure increases.
- Very large drops develop a concave lower surface and can become unstable/break apart.

## Drawing implications

The hero must never use a cartoon teardrop silhouette while falling. Small falling drops are round/near-round; larger ones are bun-like with a flattened lower side. Teardrop forms are acceptable only for water hanging from a surface under gravity, where attachment to the surface creates the tapered contact geometry.
